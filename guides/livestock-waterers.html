Livestock water

Tanks and freeze-proof waterers for cattle, goats, and poultry

By Agrivia Updated September 2026

Educational overview, not a lab ranking. We do not currently run affiliate tracking. Confirm winter electric work with a licensed electrician.

What to decide first

  • Species and head count: Six cattle need a tank and a refill you can keep up with. Eight hens need a drinker you can clean. Do not buy a 20-head energy-free fountain for four goats — those units often need enough traffic to stay open.
  • Power-out plan: Heated bases fail when the barn breaker trips. Keep a spare bucket and a way to break ice.
  • Garden hose is not a cattle system: Drip timers belong on beds. Tanks need a float, a hydrant, or a person.
  • Cleanliness: Algae, drowned mice, and manure splash make animals quit drinking. Easy dump and scrub beats a sealed gadget you cannot open.

Water is the first thing that fails on a mixed homestead: the cattle tank is skimmed with ice, the goat bucket is empty, and the poultry nipple line is frozen solid while the raised beds still have a drip timer on a clock. Fix water before you buy another charger or another bag of feed.

Extension offices publish daily intake ranges that move with heat, dry matter, and lactation. As a planning sketch only: mature cattle often drink on the order of 10–20+ gallons per head per day in heat; horses similar; goats and sheep much less per head but they still crowd a small tub; pigs drink steadily; a backyard flock drinks little by volume but dies quickly if the waterer is empty or frozen. Size for the hottest week and the coldest night, not the average Tuesday. Agrivia has not measured your well.

Tanks for cattle, horses, and mixed lots

A round stock tank with a float valve is the homestead default for a few cattle or a horse. Put it where you can dump and scrub it, on a pad that drains, away from the gate mud. Dominant animals crowd water; give enough rim that a timid heifer can drink. A tank under a roof stays cooler in summer and ices slower in winter, but it still needs a winter plan.

Automatic fountains with a valve save hauling. They also hide a stuck valve until the lot is a pond or the trough is dry. Walk them daily. Energy-free (insulated, ball-cover) drinkers use ground warmth and animal traffic. Catalogs often assume a minimum herd so the balls keep moving. Four cattle may not be enough traffic in a hard freeze — which is when people add a heater anyway.

Goats, sheep, and pigs

Goats dump buckets. They also stand in them. A heavy tub they cannot flip, mounted at a height they can reach without climbing into the garden fence, saves feed time. Sheep need water they can see and reach with wool in the way; ice on a narrow lip stops them. Pigs wreck plastic; they need a trough they cannot crush and a valve they cannot unscrew with a snout.

Do not share a poultry nipple line with goats. Do not put a cattle tank in a goat paddock without a step or a height they can use without falling in. Kids and lambs drown in deep tanks; a float that keeps a shallow pan full is safer than a deep open drum.

Poultry drinkers

Hens need clean water more than they need a clever product. Open founts dump and freeze. Heated bases under a metal fount work if you have power, a GFCI, and a cord animals cannot shred. Plastic “thermal” drinkers delay a freeze; they do not beat a week of single digits with six birds and no traffic. Nipple lines stay cleaner and freeze at the exposed pipe. Keep a backup fount you can fill with warm water when the power is out.

If the flock shares a yard with cattle, keep poultry water off the ground enough that cattle do not crush it, and keep cattle tanks where hens will not bathe and foul the only cow water.

Refill rate and wells

A garden hose that fills a tank overnight is fine if the tank is large enough to buffer a hot afternoon. It is not fine if six cattle empty it by noon and the well recovery is slow. Watch how long a fill takes. If the pump also runs the house and drip irrigation, stagger garden cycles — see the hose-timer guide. Do not size a homestead to feedlot gallons-per-minute charts for 100 cows.

Buying checklist

  • You can dump, scrub, and see the water.
  • A winter plan that does not assume the grid stays up.
  • Height and depth safe for the smallest animals (kids, lambs, calves).
  • Cords and heaters on GFCI, out of chewing range.
  • Separate from garden drip so a timer cannot starve a tank.

Questions people ask

Can I use a heated dog bowl for hens?

People do for a few birds. Check wattage, GFCI, chew-proof cords, and whether the thermostat survives a coop. Have a non-electric backup. This is not a product endorsement.

Do cattle need heated water in the South?

Many winters, no. One ice week, yes. A tank you can break ice on and a float that still works when the surface is slush is the real requirement.

Will the Agrivia chat tell me wattage?

It can talk through questions. Match heater ratings to the maker’s climate chart and an electrician. This site does not sell waterers.
