Soil pens and moisture sensors for beds and paddocks

By Agrivia Updated September 2026

Educational overview, not a lab ranking. We do not currently run affiliate tracking.

What to decide first

  • Raised beds: A cheap handheld pen plus a finger check is usually enough. Leave a probe in one representative bed if you travel.
  • Pasture or a drylot: Moisture at the surface is not the same as moisture at the roots. A probe that reaches 6–12 inches tells you more than a lawn meter.
  • Skip telemetry: LoRaWAN gateways and field radios are for large acreage. A homestead does not need them to water tomatoes or to know a paddock is baked hard.
  • One number is not a diagnosis: Clay, compost, and sandy fill read differently. Calibrate against how plants and forage actually look.

If you keep a garden and animals on the same place, you are already managing two soils. A raised bed is a box of mix you built on purpose. A paddock is native dirt, manure, and whatever the last rain did. A soil moisture pen can help both — if you buy the right length and you do not treat the display as a lab result.

This guide is for backyard plots, a few acres, and a small herd or flock. It is not a pivot telemetry spec sheet. Agrivia has not run side-by-side field trials of branded probes. Confirm odd readings with your county extension office.

Two jobs: beds versus pasture

In a four-by-eight bed, overwatering shows up as yellow lower leaves, fungus gnats, and tomatoes that split. Underwatering shows as wilt at noon that does not recover by evening. You can catch a lot of that with a finger two knuckles down, in the morning, before the sun cooks the surface. A pen is useful when beds look the same from above but one is peat-heavy and the other is sandy, or when you are gone for a weekend and a hose timer is on a clock.

In a cattle, goat, or sheep paddock the question is different. Surface crust can be dry while grass still has moisture at six inches — or the reverse after a light rain. Animals compact the gate and the waterer. Those spots stay wet and slick while the rest of the lot is dust. If you are deciding whether to move a small herd, or whether a new seeding will take, a probe that reaches the root zone is more honest than kicking the top inch.

What the common pens actually measure

Most inexpensive “soil moisture meters” sold for houseplants are resistance pens: two metal prongs, a battery or no battery, a 1–10 scale. They are sensitive to salts and to how hard you shove them in. They are fine as a relative check in the same bed over time. They are a poor way to compare a compost bed to a clay paddock.

Capacitance (often labeled FDR) probes estimate water by how the soil affects an electric field. They tend to be more stable than cheap resistance pens if the probe is seated fully and the soil around it is not full of air gaps. Tensiometers and gypsum-block “matric potential” sensors measure how hard roots must pull for water. Those are closer to what a plant feels, but they need maintenance and they are overkill for six tomato beds.

How deep to read

Tomatoes, peppers, and squash in a bed care about roughly the top 8–12 inches once they are established. Lettuce and herbs are shallower. A two-inch houseplant spike will tell you the mulch is dry and miss the wet layer underneath.

Perennial pasture and hay-type grasses often root deeper than a kitchen garden. A six-inch reading is a start; twelve inches is better if you are deciding whether a paddock is actually droughted or just dusty on top. After cattle or goats have been on a lot, take readings away from the waterer and the gate — those spots lie.

Leaving a probe versus walking around

A probe you leave in place is only as good as the spot you picked. In a garden, choose a bed that is typical, not the one against a south wall and not the one under a downspout. In a paddock, avoid the shade line of a barn if the rest of the lot is open. Mark the spot so you do not step on it or let a cow pull the cable.

Walking with a handheld pen is slower and more honest on a mixed place: one stick in the tomatoes, one in the berry row, one in the goat paddock. Write the numbers down the first week so you learn what “5” means in your mix.

Animals and sensors

Cattle, goats, and pigs will destroy anything they can reach. Do not leave a Wi-Fi spike in a lot. If you want soil data near a waterer, put the electronics on the outside of the fence and only the probe in the ground, or skip electronics and use a trowel. Poultry runs compact and add nitrogen; a pen there is mostly telling you the run is wet, which you can see from the mud.

Manure and urine change electrical readings. After a rain on a loafing area, wait. A high reading next to a hay ring is not “good moisture for grass.”

Buying checklist

  • Length: at least as deep as the crop or forage you care about, not a two-inch houseplant spike for a cattle paddock.
  • Replaceable prongs if it is a cheap resistance pen — they rust.
  • A simple 1–10 or percent display you can read in sun. You do not need a LoRaWAN dashboard.
  • If it needs an app, confirm it still shows a number when the cloud is down.
  • Price: a useful handheld pen is often in the range of a bag of feed, not a telemetry station.

Questions people ask

Can one probe run both the garden and the cattle lot?

You can carry one handheld pen everywhere. Do not compare the number in compost to the number in compacted clay and call it science. Trend each spot separately.

Do I need this if I already have a drip timer?

A timer does not know a week of rain. A pen, a rain gauge, or looking at the plants will. Soil skip on a hose timer is the next step — see the irrigation guide.

Will Agrivia’s chat diagnose my soil from a number?

No. The advisor on this site is guidance. Send a photo of the plants or forage and say what you measured. Confirm with extension if something looks like disease or a nutrient crash.
