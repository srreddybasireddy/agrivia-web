Field practice

Photo-based crop disease checks

Agrivia editorial · August 31, 2026 · Field practice, not a diagnosis tool

Clear photos help you describe symptoms to an agronomist, a retailer, or a diagnostic lab. They do not replace a person who knows your region, and they are not a lab assay. This article does not claim that Agrivia measured a yield increase, and it does not describe a store-listed in-app model.

What a useful photo set is for

You are trying to show pattern, color, and context. A specialist wants to know whether the problem is on new leaves or old, whether it follows a spray pass or a low spot, and whether lesions have a distinct margin. One tight, filtered close-up of a brown spot does almost none of that.

Phone apps and chat replies can list look-alikes. Treat those as a shortlist. Nutrient burn, herbicide drift, sunscald, and several pathogens can share a color. When the spray or the plow-down is expensive, send tissue or a physical sample the way your lab asks — photos first, sample if they say so.

What to photograph

In daylight, shoot both sides of affected leaves. Include a wide shot of the plant in the row and a still wider shot of the patch in the field. Put healthy tissue in the same frame as the damaged area when you can. That comparison is often the most useful picture in the set.

Avoid backlight, heavy HDR, and beauty filters. They hide lesion color and the edge of the spot. If the day is harsh, step to the shade side of the plant or use your body as a block. A coin, a finger, or a ruler in one frame gives scale. Wet leaves after rain glare; wait or blot.

Photograph stems, fruit, and roots if those are involved — not only the prettiest leaf. A crown rot will not show in a canopy selfie. Pull one plant if you are allowed to, rinse the roots, and shoot them against a plain board. Then bag that plant the way the lab wants if you are sending it.

Pattern in the field

Walk more than one plant. Note whether damage is on the high ridge, the wet corner, the pivot edge, the last pass of the sprayer, or the newest planting. A straight line often says equipment or chemistry. A low oval often says water. Scattered plants in an otherwise clean stand can still be seed, insect, or a first infection — do not guess from the map alone, but do send the map description with the photos.

What to write with the pictures

  • Crop, variety if known, and growth stage
  • When you first noticed it, and whether it is spreading day to day
  • Recent weather: rain, humidity, irrigation, heat, or hail
  • Where it sits in the field (low spot, new seed, fence row, pivot edge)
  • What you already applied (fertilizer, herbicide, fungicide) and when
  • Whether insects are present, and on which side of the leaf

Date-stamp is automatic on most phones. Keep the originals. Do not send only a compressed social-media upload if the lab asked for full-size files.

How to use the pictures

Compare with current extension fact sheets for your state — names and look-alikes change, and old blog photos are a poor atlas. Then confirm any spray or cultural change with someone accountable for your field. If you use the Agrivia website advisor, treat the reply as guidance and send the same set to a person who knows your county.

When the decision is a costly fungicide, a replant, or a food-safety question, ask the lab what they need. Some pathogens need a wet chamber or a PCR test. Photos get you in the right line at the counter. They do not finish the line.

Naming files so the set stays usable

Use a simple name: field, crop, date, and a short note (for example north40-corn-2026-08-31-lowspot). If three people text photos into a group chat, the set is gone in a week. One folder per problem, originals kept, is enough. You do not need a farm-management platform to start that habit.

If you later send the same case to a lab, you can attach the folder instead of hunting through a camera roll. That also keeps you from sending last year’s similar-looking leaf by mistake. If two fields look the same, keep two folders. Mixing them is how a clean block gets a spray recommendation meant for the sick one.

What people over-collect

Fifty near-identical close-ups and no field shot. Five honest frames plus notes beat a hundred blurry ones. You also do not need a new “AI scouting” subscription to start this habit. A phone you already carry and a written note in the truck will do.

Light, time of day, and repeating the set

Early morning and late afternoon are kinder than noon glare. If you must shoot at midday, keep the sun behind you and the leaf unshaded by your phone. Come back in 48 hours and take the same three distances again. A problem that doubled overnight is a different conversation from a spot that did not move. The second set is often what an agronomist thanks you for.

When photos are not enough

Skip the guess-and-spray loop if the stand is collapsing overnight, if you suspect a quarantine pest, or if the buyer of the crop requires a documented diagnosis. Call the people who own that process. Photos can travel with the sample; they should not replace it.

Questions to ask the person you send them to

  • How many photos, at what size, and to which address?
  • Do you want a physical sample, and how should I pack it?
  • What look-alikes are common in this county this week?
  • What will you not tell me from photos alone?
