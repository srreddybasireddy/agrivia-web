Fencing

Chargers for cattle, goats, sheep, and poultry

By Agrivia Updated September 2026

Educational overview, not a lab ranking. We do not currently run affiliate tracking. Follow the charger’s safety labels; this is not an installation manual.

What to decide first

  • Species first: Poultry netting, goats, and sheep need a hotter, cleaner pulse than a few cattle on a short hot wire. One undersized solar unit will not do all three jobs well.
  • Output joules, not the number on the box: Stored joules are marketing. Ask for output joules, then derate for weeds.
  • Grounding is most of the work: Dry soil and a single short rod is why a “strong” charger feels dead.
  • One energizer per fence: Never tie two chargers onto the same wire.

An electric fence is a reminder, not a wall. Cattle learn a hot wire and mostly respect it. Goats test it. Sheep insulate themselves with wool. Poultry netting has to stay tight and weed-free or the pulse collapses. Horses need a highly visible line so they do not run through it in a panic. On a homestead you may keep more than one of these animals — which means you may need more than one fence style, even if you only buy one charger sized for the hardest job.

This is for a backyard run, a few acres, and a small herd or flock — not a ten-mile commercial perimeter. Agrivia has not lab-tested chargers. Use a digital fence voltmeter. Confirm predator and stray-voltage questions with local extension or a fencing dealer who will stand in your dirt.

What “enough joules” means here

Manufacturer “miles of fence” ratings assume clean wire and good ground. Weeds, netting, and wet grass steal energy. For a short cattle paddock with one or two hot wires, a low-impedance charger in the neighborhood of 1–3 output joules is a common homestead starting point — then you measure voltage on the far corner. For goats and sheep, plan hotter and keep vegetation off the wire. For poultry netting, a unit sold for netting (often well under 2 output joules but made for that load) plus a weed-free bottom is more useful than a huge cattle charger dumping into a short, leaky net.

Voltage targets people cite from extension and fencing catalogs are roughly: cattle often held around 3,000–4,000 volts on a clean wire; horses similar but with high visibility; sheep and goats often want 4,000–5,000+ because of hair and testing; poultry nets need a pulse you can still read at the far end after grass touches the bottom. Those are planning numbers, not a guarantee. Dry sand, frozen ground, and a rusty splice will miss them.

Plug-in versus solar

If you have a grounded outlet at the barn, a plug-in energizer is simpler: no battery to replace, more consistent pulse in a week of clouds. Keep it off the rain, use the lightning kit the maker specifies, and do not share a sketchy extension cord across a puddle.

Solar makes sense on a back paddock or a chicken tractor you move. Size the panel and battery for winter sun at your latitude, not July. A unit that dies every January is not “set and forget.” Bring the battery in to charge if you only run netting in summer. Cattle on a remote lot in January need a system you have actually tested on a dark week.

Grounding

The circuit is charger → fence → animal → soil → ground rods → charger. If the rods are short, rusty, or in dry fill, the animal does not complete the circuit and walks through. A common homestead starting point is multiple rods (often 6–8 feet, spaced apart, coupled with fence-rated wire), driven where soil stays moist — not in the gravel driveway. Dry, sandy, or frozen ground needs more rod, a different placement, or a hot/ground return wire system. Test under load: a voltmeter on the ground system, following the charger maker’s method, tells you more than a guess.

Keep ground rods away from buried phone and well lines, and away from where you stand in socks. Disconnect before thunderstorms if that is your practice; follow the manual.

Gardens and mixed yards

A hot wire around a garden will stop cattle and most goats from the tomatoes if they are trained. It will not stop a determined deer by itself. Poultry netting around beds can protect plants and keep birds out of the lettuce — until weeds short it. Do not run a cattle-scale charger on a short decorative wire at kid height. Label gates. Tell visitors. Train dogs; a pet through a net is a bad afternoon.

Buying checklist

  • Output joules and a species the maker actually lists.
  • Low-impedance if you have grass load.
  • A digital voltmeter in the same cart as the charger.
  • Ground rod kit, not a tent stake.
  • Lightning protection as specified — especially on a plug-in barn unit.
  • Netting-compatible if the job is poultry; cattle-compatible if the job is a paddock. Read both labels if you share a charger.

Questions people ask

Can one charger run the cattle paddock and the chicken net?

Sometimes, if the maker allows it and the total leak is small. Often the net steals the pulse and the cattle fence goes soft. Two smaller units, or one unit and a switch, is cleaner. Never two chargers on one wire.

Will it start a grass fire?

Modern low-impedance pulses are very short. Follow the manual, keep the charger in good repair, and do not use obsolete continuous-current units. Local fire rules still apply in dry season.

Is this a substitute for woven perimeter fence?

For cattle, a hot offset on decent physical fence is common. For goats and dogs, physical fence still does most of the work. Electric is the reminder.
