Heat

Heat, shade, cattle, and flocks

By Agrivia Updated September 2026

Educational overview, not a veterinary protocol. We do not currently run affiliate tracking. Heat stroke in animals is an emergency — call a vet.

What to decide first

  • Shade and water before misters: A cloth or a tree line and a tank that stays full do more for a small herd than a fogger that raises humidity in a still lot.
  • Cattle, goats, sheep, horses, pigs, and poultry are not the same job: A drylot cow needs space under shade. Hens need airflow through a coop. Goats want a dry roof more than a mist.
  • Garden spray is not livestock cooling: The hose timer on the tomatoes should not be your cattle sprinkler.
  • Watch the animals, not only the thermometer: Panting, crowding the tank, standing in water, and birds holding wings out are the signal.

Heat on a homestead shows up in the same week the garden wilts: cattle bunch under the one tree, goats refuse the sun paddock, hens pant in a coop that was fine in April, and the tank is empty by two o’clock. You do not need a feedlot soaking system. You do need shade they can all fit under, water they can all reach, and air that moves.

University extension services use a temperature-humidity index (THI) for cattle. Planning charts often start flagging dairy-type stress in the high 60s THI and beef lots somewhat later — humidity is the multiplier. Those charts are for orientation. A black cow on a drylot in still air will suffer earlier than a chart in an office. Agrivia has not instrumented your paddock. When in doubt, add shade and water and call a vet if an animal is down or in obvious distress.

Cattle and horses on a few acres

Extension shade-area figures for feedlots (often cited around 20–50 square feet per adult depending on climate and cloth) are a starting conversation, not a building permit. For a handful of cattle, the practical test is: can they all stand in shade at once without stacking, and does the ground under the shade dry enough that it does not become a hoof pit? North–south shade structures let sun move across the floor. Trees are fine until they die or a storm takes the only oak. Cloth in the 70–80% shade range is common; too-dense cloth with no air makes a hot box.

Horses need the same logic plus fly control and a way out of a metal shed that cooks. Dark coats and heavy breeds suffer first. A run-in that faces away from afternoon sun beats a closed barn with no ridge vent.

Misters and soakers: coarse drops that wet the hide can help cattle if air moves and the lot does not turn to soup. Fine mist in still, humid weather can make it worse. Do not aim garden misters at heads. Do not leave a sprinkler on a timer overnight so the lot is mud in the morning.

Goats, sheep, and pigs

Goats handle heat better if they have a dry roof, a breeze, and water they have not dumped. They still crowd shade. A simple three-sided shed, not a plastic tarp that collects heat, is the usual homestead answer. Sheep with wool in high humidity are a different risk — shearing timing is a flock decision with a shearer or extension, not a website. Pigs have little ability to sweat; they need shade and a wallow or a wetted area you can keep sanitary. A hog in full sun on dry dirt is an emergency waiting on the clock.

Poultry

Coops fail in heat because they were built for winter. Open vents high and low so air actually moves. A dark metal roof without ridge vent cooks birds. Frozen-water problems in January become empty-water problems in July — nipples freeze then they also clog with algae. Frozen is the winter guide; heat is water plus shade on the run plus not overcrowding. Misters on a hoop can help if they do not soak bedding into ammonia. Birds holding wings out, panting, and clustering at the drinker need you now, not a new product.

Water space in heat

Intake jumps in heat. If cattle or goats have to wait on one small tub, the timid ones go without. Two smaller tanks beat one crowded rim. Poultry need more than one drinker if the flock is mixed ages. See the tanks guide for freeze and heaters; in summer the failure is empty, not ice.

Garden beds will also demand water that week. Stagger drip so the well can refill the tank. Animals drink first.

Buying checklist

  • Shade area you can measure with a tape, not a wish.
  • Cloth or a roof with air moving through, not a sealed box.
  • Water volume and rim space for the actual head count.
  • A plan for still, humid nights — that is when cattle crash.
  • Vet number on the fridge before you need it.

Questions people ask

Is THI something I must calculate?

It is a useful idea: heat plus humidity. You can also look at the animals. If they are panting and bunched on water, act.

Can I use the same shade cloth as the garden?

Garden shade cloth over tomatoes is usually smaller and lower. Cattle will walk through it or eat it if it is not on a real frame. Size and hardware to the animal.

Will Agrivia’s advisor diagnose heat stroke?

No. Guidance only. A down animal in heat is a vet call.
